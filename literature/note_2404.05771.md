## 1. Header

- **Title:** Upper bounds for the prime divisors of friends of 10
- **Authors:** Sourav Mandal (Dept. of Mathematics, Ramakrishna Mission Vivekananda
  Educational and Research Institute, Belur Math, Howrah); Sagar Mandal (Dept. of
  Mathematics and Statistics, IIT Kanpur)
- **Year:** 2024 (v1: 2024-04-07; v2: 2024-11-04)
- **arXiv:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.05771 (math.GM; MSC 11Axx, 11A25; 12 pp.)
- **Published in:** Resonance 30 (2025), 263–275. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1007/s12045-025-1747-8
- **Source read:** full text via https://arxiv.org/html/2404.05771v2 (official arXiv
  HTML, v2). Complete re-verification on 2026-08-20: statements, proof constants,
  conclusion section and bibliography (8 references) checked against the HTML.

**Notation convention in this note:** the paper uses `n` for the hypothetical friend of 10
(the project uses `N`). `q_2, q_3, q_4` = second, third and fourth smallest prime
divisors of n. `p_k` = k-th prime (p_1 = 2, p_2 = 3, ...). `log` = natural logarithm.
`ceil(x)` = ceiling function. All prime exponents of n are even (n is a perfect square,
by Ward), and the paper writes n = 5^{2a_1} · prod_{i=2}^{omega(n)} q_i^{2a_i} with primes q_i > 5.

**Context cited in the paper's introduction:** Ward (2008) [6] proved that a friend of 10 is
an odd square with at least 6 distinct prime divisors, 5 being the smallest, and with a
prime divisor ≡ 1 (mod 3). The previous paper by the same authors (arXiv:2404.00624, ref. [5])
proved omega(n) ≥ 7, the existence of primes p ≡ 1 (mod 6) and q ≡ 1 (mod 10) dividing n
(the introduction of this paper does not claim p and q are distinct; see note_2404.00624.md
for the exact statement), and the condition on the order of 5: if 5^{2a} || n, there is a
prime r | n such that 2a+1 ≡ 0 (mod f), where f is the least odd f > 1 with 5^f ≡ 1 (mod r).

## 2. Exact statements (paper's numbering)

### Main theorems (Section 1)

**Theorem 1.1.** Let n be a friend of 10 and q_2 the second smallest prime divisor of n. Then,
necessarily,

    7 <= q_2 < ceil(7·omega(n)/3) · { log(ceil(7·omega(n)/3)) + 2·log log(ceil(7·omega(n)/3)) }.

**Theorem 1.2.** Let n be a friend of 10 and q_3 the third smallest prime divisor of n. Then,
necessarily,

    11 <= q_3 < ceil(180·omega(n)/41) · { log(ceil(180·omega(n)/41)) + 2·log log(ceil(180·omega(n)/41)) }.

**Theorem 1.3.** Let n be a friend of 10 and q_4 the fourth smallest prime divisor of n. Then,
necessarily,

    13 <= q_4 < ceil(390·omega(n)/47) · { log(ceil(390·omega(n)/47)) + 2·log log(ceil(390·omega(n)/47)) }.

**IMPORTANT — stronger form embedded in the proofs:** what the proofs actually
establish is the prime-index form, which is strictly stronger:

    q_2 < p_{ceil(7·omega(n)/3)},   q_3 < p_{ceil(180·omega(n)/41)},   q_4 < p_{ceil(390·omega(n)/47)}.

The logarithmic form of the statements is obtained from this by applying Theorem 3.4
(a Rosser-type bound for p_k; applicable since ceil(7·omega/3) ≥ 4 whenever omega ≥ 2). For
computational use, ALWAYS use the prime-index form (it is much tighter; see Section 5).

The lower bounds 7, 11, 13 are trivial given Ward: the least prime of n is 5 and n is
odd with 3 ∤ n, hence q_2 ≥ 7, q_3 ≥ 11, q_4 ≥ 13.

### Preliminaries (Section 2) — properties of the abundancy index

Cited from Laatsch (1986) [3] and Weiner (2000) [7], with m, n, a positive integers:

1. I is weakly multiplicative: gcd(m,n) = 1 ⟹ I(mn) = I(m)·I(n).
2. If a > 1, then I(an) > I(n).
3. I(prod p_i^{a_i}) = prod (sum_{j=0}^{a_i} p_i^{-j}) = prod (p_i^{a_i+1} − 1)/(p_i^{a_i}(p_i − 1)).
4. If p_1,...,p_m are distinct primes, q_1,...,q_m are distinct primes, p_i <= q_i for
   every i, and t_1,...,t_m are positive integers, then
   I(prod p_i^{t_i}) >= I(prod q_i^{t_i})
   (swapping primes for smaller primes, keeping exponents, does not decrease I).
5. If n = prod p_i^{a_i}, then I(n) < prod p_i/(p_i − 1)  (strict).

Plus four elementary properties of the ceiling/floor/fractional-part functions used in the
manipulations: (i) ceil(x+n) = ceil(x)+n; (ii) ceil(x) − floor(x) = 1 if x is not an integer;
(iii) ceil(x) − floor(x) = 0 if x is an integer; (iv) {x} = x − floor(x).

### Auxiliary results (Section 3)

**Proposition 3.1.** psi(x) = x/(x−1) is strictly decreasing on (1, ∞).

**Proposition 3.2.** Omega(x) = (ax−b)/(cx−d), with a,b,c,d,alpha positive integers,
bc > ad and alpha > d/c, is strictly increasing on [alpha, ∞).
(Proof: Omega'(x) = (bc−ad)/(cx−d)² > 0.)

**Remark 3.3.** p_k > k for every k ≥ 1; hence, by Proposition 3.1,
p_k/(p_k − 1) < k/(k−1) for every k ≥ 1.

**Theorem 3.4** (cited without proof; attributed to Rosser 1939, ref. [4]). For k ≥ 4:
p_k < k·(log k + 2·log log k).
[Checked: fails at k = 3 (3·(log 3 + 2 log log 3) ≈ 3.86 < 5), holds at k = 4; numerical
verification for 4 <= k <= 2000 with no violations.]

## 3. Proof method of the main theorems

The three theorems use the same strategy, by contradiction. One writes
n = 5^{2a_1} · prod q_i^{2a_i} and assumes q_j >= p_K with K = ceil(c_j·omega(n)), where
c_2 = 7/3, c_3 = 180/41, c_4 = 390/47. By Property (4), the primes smaller than q_j are
replaced by their minimal possible values (5; then 5,7; then 5,7,11) and the primes
q_j < q_{j+1} < ... by consecutive primes p_K, p_{K+1}, ..., which only increases I;
by Property (5) and Remark 3.3 (p_k/(p_k−1) < k/(k−1)), the product telescopes:

    I(n) < C_j · (K + omega(n) − j) / (K − 1),

with C_2 = 5/4, C_3 = 5/4 · 7/6, C_4 = 5/4 · 7/6 · 11/10. Next, using the properties of
the ceiling function and splitting into cases according to the divisibility of omega(n)
(by 3, 41 and 47 respectively — the denominators of c_j; the fractional parts involved
are {omega/3}, {16·omega/41}, {14·omega/47}), the bracketed factor is bounded above by a
rational function increasing in omega(n) (Proposition 3.2) whose supremum is
(c_j + 1)/c_j (= 10/7, 221/180, 437/390). One concludes

    I(n) < 5/4 · 10/7                  = 25/14        < 9/5   (Thm 1.1)
    I(n) < 5/4 · 7/6 · 221/180         = 1547/864     < 9/5   (Thm 1.2)
    I(n) < 5/4 · 7/6 · 11/10 · 437/390 = 33649/18720  < 9/5   (Thm 1.3)

contradicting I(n) = 9/5. Hence q_j < p_K, and the logarithmic form follows from Theorem 3.4.
[I checked the three final inequalities with exact arithmetic: 5·25 = 125 < 9·14 = 126;
5·1547 = 7735 < 9·864 = 7776; 5·33649 = 168245 < 9·18720 = 168480 — correct. The
constants c_j satisfy exactly (c_j+1)/c_j < (9/5)/C_j, that is, 10/7 < 36/25,
221/180 < 216/175, 437/390 < 432/385.]

### Conclusion of the paper (Section 4)

- The authors state that they can NOT extend the method to q_5 (and beyond): they declare
  that they find no positive integers A, B with A >= B such that the method of Theorems
  1.1–1.3 proves q_5 < p_{ceil(A·omega(n)/B)}. **See the critical remark in Section 6 of this note.**
- They cite that computational search (OEIS A074902, ref. [8]) shows that the least friend
  of 10, if it exists, is strictly greater than 10^30.
- They speculate that deciding 10 may be as hard as finding an odd perfect number.

**Verdict: FAITHFUL.** Item by item:

- "upper bounds ... in terms of omega(N)" — correct: the bounds are explicit functions
  of omega(n) alone, increasing in omega(n).
- Nuance not captured (not an error, it is a summary): the form published in the
  statements is the logarithmic one; the stronger form q_j < p_{ceil(c_j·omega(n))} is in
  the proofs. The lower bounds 7 <= q_2, 11 <= q_3, 13 <= q_4 also appear in the
  statements, but are a trivial consequence of Ward.
- No omitted hypothesis: the theorems hold for any friend n of 10, with no extra
  hypothesis (the structure n = odd square with least prime 5 comes from Ward, which the
  paper uses as known; the inequalities in the proofs do not even need omega(n) ≥ 7).

## 5. Use in the project

### Immediate instantiations (Phases 1–2)

The strong form q_j < p_{ceil(c_j·omega(n))} combined with omega(N) >= 10 (item #4 of the
table, arXiv:2310.15900) does NOT directly give an absolute bound (the bounds grow with
omega), but for each fixed value omega(N) = w it gives finite ranges for q_2, q_3, q_4.
[VERIFIED-NUMERICALLY with exact integer arithmetic]:

| omega(n) = w | q_2 < p_{ceil(7w/3)} | q_3 < p_{ceil(180w/41)} | q_4 < p_{ceil(390w/47)} |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7  | q_2 < p_17 = 59  | q_3 < p_31 = 127 | q_4 < p_59 = 277 |
| 10 | q_2 < p_24 = 89  | q_3 < p_44 = 193 | q_4 < p_83 = 431 |

That is, in the omega(N) = 10 branch of the tree search (Phase 1): q_2 in {7,...,83},
q_3 in {11,...,191}, q_4 in {13,...,421} (primes, subject to the known congruences).
This prunes the first levels of the signature tree drastically. The logarithmic form is
much looser (e.g. w = 10 gives q_2 < 131.8, q_3 < 283.6, q_4 < 613.4 — natural-log
approximations, illustrative only) — always use the prime-index form.

### Reusable technique

The skeleton "minorise primes + telescope p_k/(p_k−1) < k/(k−1) + case analysis of the
ceiling function by divisibility" is elementary, 100% in exact rationals, and trivial to
implement with `Fraction`. It serves as a generic pruning routine in Phase 1: given a
signature prefix (p_1^{e_1},...,p_j^{e_j}) and a target omega, the same computation gives
the minimal index K beyond which no completion of the remaining primes reaches I = 9/5. For
Phase 3 (the 2p family): I(2p) = 3(p+1)/(2p) and the same machinery produces analogous
bounds for friends of 2p with constants depending on p — a natural candidate for a uniform lemma.

### Extensions to re-derive

## 6. Critical remark (a project finding, NOT in the paper)

**The claim in the conclusion that the method does not extend to q_5 appears to be surmountable.**
Running the same scheme for q_5 with K = 28·omega(n) (that is, A = 28, B = 1, and A >= B
as the authors themselves require):

    prefactor C_5 = 5/4 · 7/6 · 11/10 · 13/12 = 1001/576
    I(n) < 1001/576 · (29·omega − 5)/(28·omega − 1) < 1001/576 · 29/28 = 4147/2304 < 9/5,

with exact margin 9/5 − 4147/2304 = 1/11520. The fraction (29w−5)/(28w−1) is increasing in w
(Proposition 3.2 of the paper itself: bc = 5·28 = 140 > ad = 29·1 = 29) with supremum 29/28;
and the full inequality 5·1001·(29w−5) < 9·576·(28w−1), i.e. 5005(29w−5) < 5184(28w−1),
reduces to 7w + 19841 > 0, always true. This would give q_5 < p_{28·omega(n)}.
