**Conventions of this note:** sigma(N) = sum of divisors; I(N) = sigma(N)/N; omega(N) = number of distinct primes; Omega(N) = total number of prime factors (with multiplicity); p^a || N means p^a | N and p^{a+1} does not divide N; v_p(N) is the exponent of p in N (p^{v_p(N)} || N); o_q(p) = multiplicative order of p modulo q. "Friend of 10" = integer N > 10 with I(N) = I(10) = 9/5.

## 2. Exact statements (paper's numbering)

### Theorem 1.1 (Ward 2008, cited as the starting point)
If n is a friend of 10, then n is a perfect square with at least 6 distinct prime factors, the smallest being 5. Moreover, there is at least one prime p | n with p ≡ 1 (mod 3) whose exponent in the factorisation of n is ≡ 2 (mod 6); if there is exactly one such prime, its exponent is ≡ 8 (mod 18).

### Theorem 1.2 (main result)
If N is a friend of 10, then N has **at least 7 distinct prime factors** (omega(N) >= 7). Moreover, there are prime factors p and q of N, **not necessarily distinct**, such that p ≡ 1 (mod 10) and q ≡ 1 (mod 6).

### Theorem 1.3
Let p, q be distinct primes with p^{k-1} || (q - 1) for some positive integer k. Then p | sigma(q^{2a}) **if and only if** 2a + 1 ≡ 0 (mod f), where f is the smallest **odd** positive integer **> 1** such that q^f ≡ 1 (mod p^k).

- Reading remark: the statement implicitly presupposes that such an f exists. The operational form used in the rest of the paper is: if there is NO odd f > 1 with q^f ≡ 1 (mod p^k), then p does not divide sigma(q^{2a}) for any a >= 1. In the proof, f turns out to be the multiplicative order of q mod p^k (which has to be odd, since it divides the odd number 2a+1).
- Notation: f_p^q denotes the f of Theorem 1.3 for the pair (p, q).

### Corollary 1.4 (the "forced primes" tool)
Let p, p*, q be three distinct primes with p^{k-1} || (q-1) and (p*)^{k*-1} || (q-1) (k, k* positive integers); suppose p | sigma(q^{2a}) and p* | sigma(q^{2a*}) (a, a* positive integers), with f_p^q and f_{p*}^q as in Theorem 1.3. If f_{p*}^q | f_p^q, then p·p* | sigma(q^{2a}).
(In words: if p divides sigma(q^{2a}), every p* whose f divides f_p^q is forced to divide sigma(q^{2a}) as well.)

### Corollary 1.5
Let N be a friend of 10 with 5^{2a} || N, a a positive integer. Then there is a prime factor p of N such that 2a + 1 ≡ 0 (mod f) and 5^f ≡ 1 (mod p), where f is the smallest odd positive integer greater than 1 **and at most min{2a+1, p-1}**.
(The clause "f <= min{2a+1, p-1}" is a consequence of f | (2a+1) and f | (p-1) = phi(p); in the case q = 5 one has q - 1 = 4, and since p >= 7 is odd, k = 1 in Theorem 1.3, so the condition is mod p rather than mod p^k.)

### Corollary 1.6 (Fermat primes)
Let N be a friend of 10. If some Fermat prime F_k = 2^{2^k} + 1 (k >= 0 an integer) divides N, then there is a prime factor p of N with p ≡ 1 (mod 2·F_k).

### Theorem 1.7 (version without a valuation hypothesis, via the residue r)
Let p and q be distinct primes with q > p, and a a positive integer. Write q ≡ r (mod p) with 1 <= r <= p-1. Then p | sigma(q^{2a}) **if and only if**:
- case r != 1: 2a + 1 ≡ 0 (mod f), where f is the smallest odd positive integer > 1 such that r^f ≡ 1 (mod p);
- case r = 1: 2a + 1 ≡ 0 (mod p).

### Corollary 1.8
If N is a friend of 10, then N has a prime factor p ≡ 1 (mod 6).
(From 9 | sigma(N): some p | N has 3 | sigma(p^{2a_p}); r = p mod 3 = 2 is impossible since no odd f satisfies 2^f ≡ 1 (mod 3); hence p ≡ 1 (mod 3), and p being odd, p ≡ 1 (mod 6).)

### Theorem 1.9
If N = 5^{2a}·m^2 is a friend of 10, with a, m positive integers, then m is **not** squarefree.

### Theorem 1.10
If N = 5^{2a}·m^2 is a friend of 10, with a, m positive integers (that is, 5^{2a} || N), then
**Omega(N) >= 2·omega(N) + 6a - 4.**

### Corollary 1.11
If N = 5^{2a}·m^2 is a friend of 10, with a, m positive integers, and Omega(m) <= K, then
**N < 5·6^{(2^{K-2a+1} - 1)^2}.**
(The proof exhibits the chain N < 5·6^{(2^{omega(N)} - 1)^2} < 5·6^{(2^{K-2a+1}-1)^2}.)

### Relevant Remarks
- **Remark 3.1:** if N = prod_{i=1}^m p_i^{2a_i} (p_1 = 5) is a friend of 10, then sigma(5^{2a_1}) · prod_{i>=2} sigma(p_i^{2a_i}) = 9 · 5^{2a_1 - 1} · prod_{i>=2} p_i^{2a_i}. Hence (i) for every p_i there is p_j != p_i with p_i | sigma(p_j^{2a_j}); (ii) the prime factors of each sigma(p_i^{2a_i}) belong to {3, 5, p_2, ..., p_m}. This is the "master equation" behind all eliminations.
- **Remark 3.2:** since 5 | N and 5 = F_1 is a Fermat prime, Corollary 1.6 gives a prime factor p ≡ 1 (mod 10). Likewise, if 17 = F_2 | N, then N has a prime factor ≡ 1 (mod 34).
- **Remark 3.3:** in Theorem 1.7 with p = 5 and q > 5: for r in {2,3,4} there is no odd f > 1 with r^f ≡ 1 (mod 5); hence 5 | sigma(q^{2l}) forces q ≡ 1 (mod 10) and 2l + 1 ≡ 0 (mod 5).
- **Remark 3.4:** if q ≡ 1 (mod 10) then o_5(q) = 1, and if v_5(sigma(q^{2l})) = r then (Lemma 2.1) r = v_5(2l+1), so 2l + 1 ≡ 0 (mod 5^r).
- **Remark 3.5:** Theorem 1.9 gives the trivial bound Omega(N) >= 2·omega(N) + 2.
- **Remark 3.7:** from Theorem 1.10 follow Omega(m) >= omega(N) + 2a - 1 (eq. 7) and Omega(m) >= omega(m) + 2a (eq. 8).

### Auxiliary lemmas
- **Lemma 2.1** (valuation of sigma; from Nielsen/Voight): for primes p, q with q >= 3 and a >= 1:
  v_q(sigma(p^a)) = v_q(p^{o_q(p)} - 1) + v_q(a+1), if o_q(p) | (a+1) and o_q(p) != 1; = v_q(a+1), if o_q(p) = 1; = 0, otherwise.
- **Lemma 2.2:** psi(x) = a·x - a^x is strictly decreasing on [1, infinity) for every a > e.
- **Lemma 2.3:** if (c_1, ..., c_k) is a partition of n (sum of the c_i = n, 1 <= k <= n, c_i >= 1) and a > e is an integer, then a·n < sum_{i=1}^k a^{c_i}. **[Warning: as written, it is false in the limiting case where all c_i = 1, where EQUALITY a·n = sum a^{c_i} holds; the correct statement is a·n <= sum a^{c_i}, with equality iff all c_i = 1. See "slips" below — it does not affect the final result.]**
- **Definition 2.4:** odd M is "odd m/d-perfect" if sigma(M)/M = m/d.
- **Lemma 2.5** (Nielsen 2015): if M is odd m/d-perfect with k distinct prime factors, then M < d·(d+1)^{(2^k - 1)^2}.
- **Combinatorial definitions:** E_q(x) = {p odd prime : p | x, q | sigma(p^eta), eta >= 2 even}; A_{n,a}(r) = { sum_{i=1}^r a^{c_i} - r : sum c_i = n, c_i >= 1 }; L_{n,a} = union of A_{n,a}(r) for r = 1..n.
- **Lemma 3.6:** the least element of L_{2a-1, 5} is 8a - 4 (attained by the all-ones partition, r = 2a-1). [VERIFIED-NUMERICALLY: a = 1..5, exhaustive search over partitions.]

## 3. Proof methods

- **Theorem 1.3 / Theorem 1.7:** elementary manipulation of sigma(q^{2a}) = (q^{2a+1} - 1)/(q - 1) and multiplicative orders. For 1.3: p | sigma(q^{2a}) with p^{k-1} || (q-1) forces q^{2a+1} ≡ 1 (mod p^k); the order f of q mod p^k divides 2a+1 (odd), so f is odd and > 1 (f = 1 would give p^k | q-1, contradiction). The converse reverses the argument using gcd(q-1, p^k) = p^{k-1}. For 1.7 the same reasoning is carried out with the residue r of q mod p, separating the degenerate case r = 1 (where sigma(q^{2a}) ≡ 2a+1 mod p).
- **Theorem 1.2:** exhaustive case analysis assuming omega(N) = 6 (Ward excludes <= 5). With p_1 = 5 fixed, the growth constraints on I (I(N) < prod p_i/(p_i - 1), monotonicity) leave only 19 possible "chains" (p_2, ..., p_6). Each chain is eliminated by: (a) Remark 3.2 — if 17 | N there must be a prime ≡ 1 (mod 34), which the chain lacks; (b) Remark 3.1 — some prime must divide sigma(5^{2a_1}), but via Corollary 1.4 this drags in a companion prime p* (e.g. 11 | sigma(5^{2a}) => 71 | sigma(5^{2a}), since f_11^5 = f_71^5 = 5; 19 => 829, since f_19^5 = 9 and f_829^5 = 9) that does not belong to the chain; (c) exact comparison of fractions: I(N) >= I(chain with exponents 2) > 9/5. Case 13 (5,7,11,13,29,p_6 with 31 <= p_6 <= 20731) requires combined congruences (p_6 ≡ 1, 61 or 211 (mod 390)) and three tables of forced primes p* for each candidate p_6. The part "p ≡ 1 (mod 10) and q ≡ 1 (mod 6)" follows from Remark 3.2 and Corollary 1.8, with no case analysis.
- **Corollary 1.6:** by contradiction: if no prime factor of N is ≡ 1 (mod F_k), then k* = 1 and f would have to be an odd divisor > 1 of phi(F_k) = 2^{2^k}, which does not exist; hence F_k divides no sigma(p^{2a_p}), contradicting Remark 3.1.
- **Theorem 1.9:** if m were squarefree, N = 5^{2a} · prod p_j^2; the master equation requires 5 | sigma(p_t^2) for some t (since 5^{2a-1} >= 5 divides the right-hand side and gcd(5, sigma(5^{2a})) = 1); but 5 | sigma(p_t^2) would require 3 = 2·1 + 1 ≡ 0 (mod 5) (Remarks 3.3/3.4), absurd.
- **Theorem 1.10:** bookkeeping of the 5-adic valuation: 5^{2a-1} has to be distributed among the sigma(p_i^{2gamma_i}) with p_i ≡ 1 (mod 10), and v_5(sigma(p_i^{2gamma_i})) = v_i implies 2gamma_i + 1 ≡ 0 (mod 5^{v_i}), hence 2gamma_i >= 5^{v_i} - 1. Minimising sum 2gamma_i subject to sum v_i = 2a - 1 is minimising an element of L_{2a-1,5}; by Lemma 3.6 the minimum is 8a - 4 (the all-ones partition: 2a - 1 distinct primes ≡ 1 (mod 10), each with v_5 = 1 and exponent >= 4). The remaining primes contribute exponent >= 2 each; the balance gives Omega(N) >= 2·omega(N) + 6a - 4.
- **Corollary 1.11:** from Omega(m) <= K and Remark 3.7 (eq. 7) comes omega(N) <= K - 2a + 1; N is odd 9/5-perfect, and Lemma 2.5 (Nielsen 2015) with d = 5 gives N < 5·6^{(2^{omega(N)} - 1)^2}.

### Slips / rigour remarks detected during the reading (none fatal)
1. **Lemma 2.3 with strict inequality:** for the all-ones partition equality a·n = sum a^{c_i} holds (e.g. 5·1 = 5^1); the statement should read "<=". Lemma 3.6 remains correct: the minimum of L_{2a-1,5} is indeed 8a - 4, ATTAINED by the all-ones partition (verified exhaustively for a = 1..5), and therefore the conclusion of Theorem 1.10 is unaffected.
2. **Remark 3.7 (eq. 7) — off-by-one in the displayed derivation [DIVERGENCE VERIFIED
   INDEPENDENTLY in this Phase 0, on the v5 text via arxiv.org/html/2404.00624v5]:** the
   paper writes "Ω(5^{2a}) + Ω(m²) = 2a + 2Ω(m) ≥ 2ω(N) + 6a − 4" and concludes eq. (7)
   "Ω(m) ≥ ω(N) + 2a − 1". But the displayed line gives 2Ω(m) ≥ 2ω(N) + 4a − 4, i.e.
   **Ω(m) ≥ ω(N) + 2a − 2** — one less. There is no parity gain (both sides of the
   starting inequality are even), so eq. (7) would require a genuine improvement of
   Theorem 1.10 (Ω(N) ≥ 2ω(N) + 6a − 3), which the paper does not prove. Eq. (8) and
   **Corollary 1.11 inherit the problem**: the proof, as written ("Since Ω(m) ≤ K, we have
   from (7) that K − 2a + 1 ≥ ω(N)"), supports only ω(N) ≤ K − 2a + 2 and therefore
   only **N < 5·6^{(2^{K−2a+2}−1)²}**. The original statement may still be true, but
   until an independent proof exists the project must use ONLY the corrected (weaker)
   version. Also recorded in results/PHASE_0.md §10.
3. **Typo in Case 12 of Theorem 1.2:** the printed factor 381/361 is I(19^2), but the prime of the chain is 23; the correct value is I(23^2) = 553/529. Redoing it with exact arithmetic: I(5^4·7^2·11^2·13^2·23^2) = 1111642101/614631875 > 9/5 and I(5^2·7^2·11^2·13^2·23^2·31^2) = 15547332483/8383578775 > 9/5 — the conclusion of the case stands. ## 4. Fidelity to the overview table (docs/METHODOLOGY.md)

Table summary (row #3): "omega(N) >= 7; there are primes p ≡ 1 (mod 10) and q ≡ 1 (mod 6) dividing N; if 5^{2a} || N, there is a prime p | N with 2a+1 ≡ 0 (mod f), where f is the least odd f > 1 with 5^f ≡ 1 (mod p); an extra condition if a Fermat prime divides N; in N = 5^{2a}·m^2, m is not squarefree".

Item by item:

1. **"omega(N) >= 7"** — FAITHFUL (Theorem 1.2).
2. **"there are primes p ≡ 1 (mod 10) and q ≡ 1 (mod 6) dividing N"** — FAITHFUL, with one omitted nuance: the paper makes explicit that p and q are **not necessarily distinct** (a single prime ≡ 1 (mod 30) could play both roles). Since the table does not assert distinctness, there is no error; but when using the constraint in prime counts (e.g. combining with omega >= 10), one must NOT assume two distinct primes from this statement.
3. **"if 5^{2a} || N, there is a prime p | N with 2a+1 ≡ 0 (mod f), where f is the least odd f > 1 with 5^f ≡ 1 (mod p)"** — FAITHFUL (Corollary 1.5). The table omits the paper's clause "f <= min{2a+1, p-1}", but that clause is redundant (it follows from f | (2a+1) and f | (p-1)); the mathematical content is identical.
4. **"an extra condition if a Fermat prime divides N"** — FAITHFUL but vague; the exact statement (Corollary 1.6) is: if the Fermat prime F_k | N, then N has a prime factor p ≡ 1 (mod 2·F_k). Worth recording that "p ≡ 1 (mod 10)" in item 2 is exactly this corollary with F_1 = 5.
5. **"in N = 5^{2a}·m^2, m is not squarefree"** — FAITHFUL (Theorem 1.9).

**Divergence (by omission):** the table does NOT mention two quantitative results of the paper (present in versions v3–v5, Jan 2025, and in the current abstract):
- **Theorem 1.10:** Omega(N) >= 2·omega(N) + 6a - 4 (with 5^{2a} || N) — stronger than the trivial Omega(N) >= 2·omega(N) + 2;
- **Corollary 1.11:** if Omega(m) <= K then N < 5·6^{(2^{K-2a+1}-1)^2} (the first conditional UPPER bound for a friend of 10 in this line of literature).
Recommendation: add both to row #3 of the table.

No item of the table is stronger than what the paper proves (no overstatement); the omissions above are the only deviation.

## 5. Use in the project

**Results to re-derive/verify (Phases 0–1):**
- Theorems 1.3 and 1.7 are elementary and short — full re-derivation recommended; they are the basis of ALL congruence constraints in this niche. Lemma 2.1 (valuation of sigma) already covers and generalises both and should go into `core/` with an exhaustive test on small ranges.
- Corollary 1.5 + Remark 3.4 give strong constraints on the exponent of 5: if v_5(sigma(p^{2gamma})) = r then 2gamma + 1 ≡ 0 (mod 5^r) — exponents of primes ≡ 1 (mod 10) grow exponentially with the 5-adic valuation they carry. This is direct pruning in the Phase 1 tree search (propagation of divisibilities via 5·sigma(N) = 9·N).
- Theorem 1.10 and Remark 3.7 are signature prunings: given (omega, a) fixed in the tree, a known minimum Omega; combine with the lower bound N > 625·9^{omega(N)-3} (arXiv:2504.08295) and with omega >= 10 (arXiv:2310.15900). With omega(N) >= 10 and a >= 1, Theorem 1.10 gives Omega(N) >= 22 already in the weakest case.
- Corollary 1.11 deserves attention in Phase 2: it is a CONDITIONAL upper bound (depends on Omega(m) <= K). Crossing it with unconditional lower bounds can eliminate ranges of (a, K): if 5·6^{(2^{K-2a+1}-1)^2} <= current lower bound, then Omega(m) > K is forced — one more structural constraint for free.

**Reusable techniques:**
- **Corollary 1.4 ("forced primes"):** if p | sigma(q^{2a}) then every p* with f_{p*}^q | f_p^q also divides sigma(q^{2a}). A cheap, certifiable propagation rule (multiplicative orders only) — implement as a generic pruning rule in the Phase 1 search and as an automatic generator of tables like Tables 1–4 of the paper.
- **Master equation (Remark 3.1):** the prime factors of each sigma(p_i^{2a_i}) must belong to {3, 5, p_2, ..., p_m} — this is the closure test that kills almost every chain. It generalises immediately to friends of 2p (I(N) = sigma(2p)/(2p) = 3(p+1)/(2p)): Phase 3 should try to formulate the version of the master equation + Corollary 1.6 uniform in p (note that 5 being a Fermat prime is what yields "p ≡ 1 (mod 10)"; for general 2p the analogue depends on the factorisation of p-1 and on the structure of sigma(p^{2a}) — a natural candidate for a uniform lemma).
- **Valuation bookkeeping (proof of Theorem 1.10):** the scheme "distribute v_q(right-hand side) among the sigma(p_i^{2gamma_i}) and minimise the exponent sum via partitions + growth q^{v}" is generic in q; for the 2p family the same machine gives Omega(N) >= 2·omega(N) + (function of v_p) — a candidate uniform result for Phase 3.
- **Methodological warning:** the proof of Theorem 1.2 (19 chains, tables of p*) is exactly the kind of case analysis this project wants to CERTIFY by reproducible exact computation rather than trust to hand-made tables; the two slips found (strict Lemma 2.3, typo 381/361) reinforce this. The spot-check script from this reading: exact arithmetic with `fractions.Fraction`, checks Cases 10–12, the minima of L_{2a-1,5} (a <= 5) and a sample of Table 4 — to be promoted to `experiments/` in Phase 0 if we use Theorem 1.2 as a hypothesis.
