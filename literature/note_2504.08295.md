## 2. Notation and imported facts

- `F` denotes a hypothetical **friend of 10**: `F ≠ 10` and `I(F) = 9/5`, i.e. `5·σ(F) = 9·F`.
- By the facts imported from Ward 2008 [9], `F` is odd, a perfect square, with 5 as least prime divisor. The paper writes:
  - `F = 5^{2a_1} · ∏_{i=2}^{ω(F)} p_i^{2a_i}`, distinct primes with `p_1 = 5` and `p_i ≥ 7` for `i ≥ 2`, all `a_i ≥ 1`; or
  - `F = 5^{2a} · Q²`, with `a = a_1 ≥ 1` and `Q = ∏_{i=2}^{ω(F)} p_i^{a_i}` odd and coprime to 15 (hence coprime to 30).
- Facts from the literature used: `ω(F) ≥ 6` (Ward [9]); `ω(F) ≥ 7` and `Q` not squarefree (Chatterjee–Mandal–Mandal, arXiv:2404.00624 [4]); Theorem 2.2 below is cited from [4]; upper bounds for prime divisors of F come from [5], [6].
- **Caution:** the paper **neither uses nor mentions** `ω(F) ≥ 10` (arXiv:2310.15900). All results hold without that hypothesis; combining them with `ω ≥ 10` is our own work (see §6).

## 3. Main results (paper's numbering; faithful paraphrase)

All statements are conditional on "F is a friend of 10" — structural constraints on a hypothetical friend.

**Theorem 1.1.** If `F = 5^{2a_1} · ∏_{i=2}^{ω(F)} p_i^{2a_i}` is a friend of 10, then it does **not** happen that all the `a_i` (for `i = 1, …, ω(F)`, **including** `a_1`) are `≡ 13 (mod 27)`.

**Theorem 1.2.** If `F = 5^{2a_1} · ∏_{i=2}^{ω(F)} p_i^{2a_i}` is a friend of 10, then it does **not** happen that all the `a_i` (for `i = 1, …, ω(F)`, **including** `a_1`) are `≡ 1 (mod 3)`.

> Reading remark (mine, not the paper's): since `13 ≡ 1 (mod 3)`, Theorem 1.2 **logically implies** Theorem 1.1. The paper presents 1.1 first as an independent and simpler argument (it uses a single auxiliary prime, 19; 1.2 uses a chain of four primes).

**Theorem 1.3.** If `F = 5^{2a} · Q²` is a friend of 10, then `σ(5^{2a}) + σ(Q²) ≡ 6 (mod 8)` **if and only if** `a` is even.

**Theorem 1.4.** If `F = 5^{2a} · Q²` is a friend of 10, then `σ(5^{2a}) + σ(Q²) ≡ 2 (mod 8)` **if and only if** `a` is odd.

> Together, 1.3 and 1.4 say: for a friend of 10, `σ(5^{2a}) + σ(Q²) mod 8 ∈ {2, 6}` always, and the parity of `a` decides which case occurs. (Sanity check by me: from `5·σ(F) = 9·F` follows `σ(5^{2a})·σ(Q²) ≡ 5 (mod 8)`, since `Q² ≡ 1` and `9·5^{2a−1} ≡ 5 (mod 8)`; combining with Proposition 2.3 the four cases of `a mod 4` reproduce exactly 6, 2, 6, 2.)

**Theorem 1.5.** If `F = 5^{2a_1} · ∏_{i=2}^{ω(F)} p_i^{2a_i}` is a friend of 10, then
`F > (25/81) · ∏_{i=1}^{ω(F)} (2a_i + 1)²`.
The product runs over **all** indices, including `i = 1`. Since `∏(2a_i+1) = d(F)` (number of divisors), the statement is equivalent to `F > (25/81) · d(F)²`.

**Corollary 1.6.** If `F` is a friend of 10, then `F > 625 · 9^{ω(F) − 3}`.

## 4. Tools (Section 2 of the paper)

**Lemma 2.1** (elementary properties of the abundancy index):
(a) `I` is weakly multiplicative: `gcd(F,G) = 1 ⇒ I(FG) = I(F)·I(G)`;
(b) if `α > 1` is an integer, then `I(αF) > I(F)`;
(c) `I(∏ p_i^{α_i}) = ∏ (p_i^{α_i+1} − 1) / (p_i^{α_i}(p_i − 1)) = ∏ ∑_{j=0}^{α_i} p_i^{−j}`;
(d) if `p_i ≤ q_i` (distinct primes within each product) then `I(∏ p_i^{l_i}) ≥ I(∏ q_i^{l_i})`;
(e) `I(∏ p_i^{α_i}) < ∏ p_i/(p_i − 1)`.

**Theorem 2.2** (cited from [4] = arXiv:2404.00624, Theorem 1.3 there). Let `p, q` be distinct primes with `p^{k−1} ∥ (q − 1)` for some positive integer `k`. Then
`p | σ(q^{2a})  ⟺  2a + 1 ≡ 0 (mod f)`,
where `f` is the **least odd integer > 1** such that `q^f ≡ 1 (mod p^k)`.
(Note: if `p ∤ (q−1)` then `k = 1` and the condition is about the order of `q` mod `p`.)

**Proposition 2.3.** For every integer `a ≥ 1`:
`σ(5^{2a}) ≡ 1 (mod 8)` if `a ≡ 0 (mod 4)`; `≡ 7` if `a ≡ 1`; `≡ 5` if `a ≡ 2`; `≡ 3` if `a ≡ 3 (mod 4)`.
(Sanity: `σ(5²)=31≡7`, `σ(5⁴)=781≡5`, `σ(5⁶)=19531≡3`, `σ(5⁸)=488281≡1`. Checks out.)

**Remark 2.4.** If `F` is a friend of 10, then `σ(F) = σ(5^{2a_1})·∏_{i≥2} σ(p_i^{2a_i}) = 9 · 5^{2a_1−1} · ∏_{i≥2} p_i^{2a_i}`, and since `3 ∤ F` (no `p_i` is 3), it follows that `9 ∥ σ(F)` — the 3-adic valuation of `σ(F)` is **exactly** 2. This fact is the engine of Theorems 1.1 and 1.2.

## 5. Proof methods (summary)

- **Theorem 1.1.** Assume all `a_i ≡ 13 (mod 27)`, i.e. `27 | 2a_i + 1`. Theorem 2.2 with `(p,q) = (19,5)`: here `f = 9` (the order of 5 mod 19 is 9; I checked: `5⁹ ≡ 1`, `5³ ≡ 11 (mod 19)`), and `9 | 27 | 2a_1+1`, hence `19 | σ(5^{2a_1}) | σ(F)`, whence `19 | F`. For the exponent `2a_j` of 19, the paper uses `19^{27} ≡ 1 (mod 486)` (`486 = 2·3⁵`): since `27 | 2a_j+1`, one has `3⁵ | 19^{2a_j+1} − 1`, and `v_3(σ(19^{2a_j})) = v_3(19^{2a_j+1}−1) − v_3(18) ≥ 5 − 2 = 3`, i.e. `27 | σ(F)` — contradiction with `9 ∥ σ(F)` (Remark 2.4).
- **Theorem 1.2.** Assume all `a_i ≡ 1 (mod 3)`, i.e. `3 | 2a_i + 1`. A chain of applications of Theorem 2.2, all with `f = 3` (orders checked by me): `(31,5)`: `5³ ≡ 1 (mod 31)` ⇒ `31 | σ(5^{2a_1})` ⇒ `31 | F`; `(3,31)` with `k=2`: `31³ ≡ 1 (mod 9)` ⇒ `3 | σ(31^{2a})`; `(331,31)`: `31³ ≡ 1 (mod 331)` ⇒ `331 | F`; `(7,331)`: `331³ ≡ 1 (mod 7)` ⇒ `7 | F`; `(3,7)` with `k=2`: `7³ ≡ 1 (mod 9)` ⇒ `3 | σ(7^{2a})`. The three factors `σ(31^{2a})`, `σ(331^{2a})`, `σ(7^{2a})` give `27 | σ(F)` — contradiction with `9 ∥ σ(F)`.
- **Theorems 1.3/1.4.** From `5σ(F) = 9F`: `σ(5^{2a})·σ(Q²) = 9·5^{2a−1}·Q² ≡ 5 (mod 8)`. Combine with Proposition 2.3 and case analysis on the units mod 8 (in both directions of the "if and only if"; the converses fall to contradictions of the type `1 ≡ 5 (mod 8)`).
- **Theorem 1.5.** AM–GM on each prime-power factor: `σ(p^{2a}) = ∑_{j=0}^{2a} p^j > (2a+1)·p^a` (pairing `p^j` with `p^{2a−j}`). Multiplying: `σ(F) > d(F)·√F`. Since `σ(F) = (9/5)F`, isolate `√F > (5/9)·d(F)` and square.
- **Corollary 1.6.** By Theorem 1.2, not all `a_i = 1` (otherwise all `≡ 1 (mod 3)`); hence some `a_j ≥ 2`, with `(2a_j+1)² ≥ 25`, and the remaining factors are `≥ 9`. Substituting into Theorem 1.5: `F > (25/81)·25·9^{ω(F)−1} = 625·9^{ω(F)−3}`.

## 6. Fidelity to the overview table (docs/METHODOLOGY.md)

Table summary (row #7): *"Not all halved exponents ≡ 1 (mod 3); conditions mod 8 on σ(5^{2a})+σ(Q²); N > (25/81)·∏(2a_i+1)², in particular N > 625·9^{ω(N)−3}"*.

Item by item:

1. **"Not all halved exponents ≡ 1 (mod 3)"** = Theorem 1.2. **FAITHFUL.** The `a_i` are exactly the halves of the exponents `2a_i`, and the statement includes `a_1` (the exponent of 5). The table omits Theorem 1.1 (the mod 27 variant), but it is implied by 1.2 — an omission with no loss of content.
2. **"conditions mod 8 on σ(5^{2a})+σ(Q²)"** = Theorems 1.3 + 1.4. **FAITHFUL** as a summary; the precise form is biconditional: sum `≡ 6 (mod 8) ⟺ a` even, and `≡ 2 (mod 8) ⟺ a` odd.
3. **"N > (25/81)·∏(2a_i+1)²"** = Theorem 1.5. **FAITHFUL** (product over all `i = 1, …, ω(N)`).
4. **"in particular N > 625·9^{ω(N)−3}"** = Corollary 1.6. **FAITHFUL** as a statement. Derivation nuance: the corollary does **not** follow from Theorem 1.5 with `a_i ≥ 1` alone (that would give only `N > 25·9^{ω−2} = 225·9^{ω−3}`); it also uses Theorem 1.2 to guarantee some `a_j ≥ 2`. The table's "in particular" is acceptable, but the corollary depends on 1.5 **and** 1.2.

**Verdict: FAITHFUL** (no divergence of statement; only the two nuances above).

## 7. Use in the project

- **Phases 0/1 (reproduction):**
  - Re-derive Remark 2.4 (`9 ∥ σ(F)`) and Proposition 2.3 — short and easy; good tests for `core/`.
  - Implement **Theorem 2.2 as a reusable routine** (`p | σ(q^{2a}) ⟺ f | 2a+1`, with `f` computed by exact multiplicative order): it is exactly the mechanism "propagate the divisibilities imposed by `5σ(N) = 9N`" planned for the Phase 1 tree search.
  - Theorem 1.5 in the form `F > (25/81)·d(F)²` is a **cheap pruning criterion**: at any node of the signature tree `(p_i, e_i)`, if `(25/81)·∏(2a_i+1)²` already exceeds the branch ceiling, prune. Cost: integers only.
  - Numerical verification: any partial candidate `5^{2a}·Q²` the search produces must satisfy 1.3/1.4 (free mod 8 check).
- **Phase 1 (bounds):** Corollary 1.6 combined with `ω(F) ≥ 10` (arXiv:2310.15900, which the paper does not use) gives only `F > 625·9⁷ ≈ 2.99×10⁹` — very weak next to the computational bounds (Section 3 of the paper cites that, for the suspected solitary numbers up to 372, a possible least friend exceeds `10^{30}`). The practical value of 1.5/1.6 is in structural pruning, not in the absolute bound.
- **Phase 2 (new constraints):** the technique of Theorems 1.1/1.2 is **systematisable**: `v_3(σ(F)) = 2` exactly (and, trivially, `v_5(σ(F)) = 2a_1 − 1`); any assignment of congruence classes to the `a_i` that, via chains of Theorem 2.2 (and LTE for high valuations), forces `v_3(σ(F)) ≥ 3` is forbidden. Concrete programme: computationally catalogue all chains `(p, q, f)` with small `f` among small primes and look for forbidden combinations of exponent residues — a potential source of new results of the same kind (before claiming novelty, check the 2024–2026 papers of S. Mandal, S. Mandal and T. Chatterjee, per the project rule).
- **Phase 3 (the 2p family):** the ingredients use little of "10" itself: for a friend `N` of `2p` one has `2p·σ(N) = 3(p+1)·N`, which likewise fixes the valuations of `σ(N)` at small primes; the pair "exact valuation of `σ(N)` + Theorem 2.2" is a natural candidate for a lemma **uniform in p**. The mod 8 arguments (an analogue of Prop 2.3 for `σ(p^{2a})`) also carry over.
