## 2. Notation and facts used by the paper

- `n` a friend of 10 ⟺ `n ≠ 10` and `I(n) = σ(n)/n = 9/5`.
- Cited facts (not re-proved here): `n` is a perfect square, least prime divisor 5 (hence `2, 3 ∤ n`) [Ward]; `ω(n) ≥ 10` [Thackeray]. In the proof the paper writes `n = 5^{2a_1} · ∏_{2≤i≤ω(n)} q_i^{2a_i}`, with `q_1 = 5 < q_2 < q_3 < …` the prime divisors of `n` in increasing order and all exponents even.
- `q_r` = r-th **smallest** prime divisor of `n`; `p_i` = i-th prime (`p_1 = 2, p_2 = 3, p_3 = 5, p_4 = 7, …`); `⌈·⌉` = ceiling; `log` = natural logarithm.
- Consequence of `2, 3 ∤ n` and `q_1 = 5`: `q_2 ≥ 7 = p_4`, `q_3 ≥ 11 = p_5`, and in general `q_j ≥ p_{j+2}` for `j ≥ 2`.

## 3. Exact statements

### Theorem 1.1 (reproduction of [4] = arXiv:2404.05771, Theorems 1, 2, 3)

If `n` is a friend of 10 and `q_2, q_3, q_4` are the 2nd, 3rd and 4th smallest prime divisors of `n`, then:

- `7 ≤ q_2 < ⌈7ω(n)/3⌉ · { log(⌈7ω(n)/3⌉) + 2·log log(⌈7ω(n)/3⌉) }`
- `11 ≤ q_3 < ⌈180ω(n)/41⌉ · { log(⌈180ω(n)/41⌉) + 2·log log(⌈180ω(n)/41⌉) }`
- `13 ≤ q_4 < ⌈390ω(n)/47⌉ · { log(⌈390ω(n)/47⌉) + 2·log log(⌈390ω(n)/47⌉) }`

### Theorem 1.2 (main result)

Let `n` be a friend of 10 and `q_r` (with `r ≥ 2`) the r-th smallest prime divisor of `n`. Then

`q_r < L · { log L + 2·log(log L) }`,   where   `L = ⌈A·ω(n)/B⌉`,

for **any** positive integers `A, B` satisfying ALL of the conditions:

1. `A/B > 1 / ( (36/25) · ∏_{4≤i≤r+1} (1 − 1/p_i)  −  1 )`
   (the product runs over the primes `p_4 = 7` up to `p_{r+1}`; for `r = 2` the product is empty = 1);
2. `gcd(A, B) = 1`;
3. `A/B ∈ Q⁺ \ Z⁺` (i.e. `B ≥ 2`, a non-integer fraction);
4. `A·B·(r−2) + 2A + B > B²`.

**Implicit sharper form (appears only in the proof, not in the statement):** under the same hypotheses, `q_r < p_L` (the L-th prime). The logarithmic form is obtained from it via Theorem 2.1 (Rosser). For computational use, `q_r < p_L` is strictly stronger.

### Remark 1.1

Since `A > B > 1` (a consequence of the conditions, in the regime where the threshold of condition 1 is > 1 — see §5), condition 4 is redundant for `r ≥ 3`; for `r = 2` it reduces to `2A + B > B²`.

### Corollary 1.1 (improves the constants of [4] for q_3 and q_4)

If `n` is a friend of 10:

- `11 ≤ q_3 < ⌈427ω(n)/100⌉ · { log(⌈427ω(n)/100⌉) + 2·log log(⌈427ω(n)/100⌉) }`
- `13 ≤ q_4 < ⌈41ω(n)/5⌉ · { log(⌈41ω(n)/5⌉) + 2·log log(⌈41ω(n)/5⌉) }`

Since `427/100 < 180/41` and `41/5 < 390/47` (verified exactly — see §5), and the expression `⌈x⌉(log⌈x⌉ + 2 log log⌈x⌉)` is increasing in `x` for `x > 3`, these bounds are strictly better than those of Theorem 1.1. The improvement margins are minimal: `180/41 − 427/100 = 293/4100` and `390/47 − 41/5 = 23/235` in the coefficient of `ω(n)`.

### Properties of the abundancy index (Section 2 of the paper; no lemma numbering)

1. `I` is weakly multiplicative: `gcd(F, G) = 1 ⟹ I(FG) = I(F)·I(G)`.
2. If `α, F ∈ Z⁺` and `α > 1`, then `I(αF) > I(F)`.
3. `I(∏_{i=1}^{n} p_i^{α_i}) = ∏_{i=1}^{n} (Σ_{j=0}^{α_i} p_i^{−j}) = ∏_{i=1}^{n} (p_i^{α_i+1} − 1)/(p_i^{α_i}(p_i − 1))`.
4. If `p_1, …, p_n` and `q_1, …, q_n` are distinct primes (within each list) with `p_i ≤ q_i` for every `i`, then, for positive exponents `l_i`: `I(∏ p_i^{l_i}) ≥ I(∏ q_i^{l_i})` (swapping primes for smaller ones only increases the index).
5. `I(∏_{i=1}^{n} p_i^{α_i}) < ∏_{i=1}^{n} p_i/(p_i − 1)` (strict).

### Theorem 2.1 (cited from Rosser 1939, [7])

If `p_n` is the n-th prime, then `p_n < n(log n + 2·log log n)` for `n ≥ 4`.
(The paper ensures `L ≥ 4` by noting that `A > B` and `ω(n) > 3` imply `⌈Aω(n)/B⌉ > 3`.)

## 4. Proof method

**Theorem 1.2** (contradiction + exact monotonicity):
1. Assume `q_r ≥ p_L`. Using `q_2 ≥ p_4, …, q_{r−1} ≥ p_{r+1}` and Properties 4 and 5, bound
   `I(n) < (5/4) · ∏_{4≤j≤r+1} p_j/(p_j−1) · ∏_{r≤i≤ω(n)} p_{L+i−r}/(p_{L+i−r}−1)`.
2. Apply `p_k/(p_k−1) ≤ k/(k−1)` (equivalent to `p_k ≥ k`; the paper credits Remark 3.3 of [4]) and telescope the tail product:
   `∏_{r≤i≤ω(n)} (L+i−r)/(L+i−r−1) = (L+ω(n)−r)/(L−1)`.
3. Prove that `(L+ω(n)−r)/(L−1) < (A+B)/A` for every `ω(n) ∈ Z⁺`, writing `A = Bk + μ` (`1 ≤ μ ≤ B−1`, `gcd(B,μ)=1`, possible since `A/B ∉ Z`) and splitting into two cases: `B ∤ ω(n)` (controls the fractional part `{μω(n)/B} ∈ {1/B, …, (B−1)/B}` and shows that the function `φ(t) = ((A+B)t + (1−r)B − 1)/(At − B + 1)` is strictly increasing with limit `(A+B)/A` — condition 4 is exactly `φ' > 0`) and `B | ω(n)` (likewise with `τ(t) = ((A+B)t − Br)/(At − B)`, automatically increasing for `r ≥ 2`, `A > B`).
4. Conclude `I(n) < (5/4) · ∏_{4≤j≤r+1} p_j/(p_j−1) · (1 + B/A)`. Condition 1 gives `1 + B/A < (36/25)·∏_{4≤i≤r+1}(1 − 1/p_i)`, and the exact identity `(5/4) · ∏ p_j/(p_j−1) · (36/25) · ∏ (1 − 1/p_i) = (5/4)(36/25) = 9/5` closes the contradiction with `I(n) = 9/5`. Hence `q_r < p_L`, and Rosser gives the statement.

**Theorem 1.1 (alternative proof)** — an instance of 1.2: `(A,B) = (7,3)` for `q_2` (threshold `25/11`), `(180,41)` for `q_3` (threshold `175/41`), `(390,47)` for `q_4` (threshold `385/47`).

**Corollary 1.1** — an instance of 1.2 with fractions closer to the threshold: `(427,100)` for `q_3` (`427/100 − 175/41 = 7/4100`) and `(41,5)` for `q_4` (`41/5 − 385/47 = 2/235`).

## 5. Independent verification (exact arithmetic, Fraction/sympy)

Threshold of condition 1: `T_r = 1/(X_r − 1)` with `X_r = (36/25) · ∏_{4≤i≤r+1}(1 − 1/p_i)`:

| r | X_r (exact) | X_r − 1 | threshold T_r | paper's choice |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 36/25 | 11/25 | 25/11 ≈ 2.2727 | 7/3 ≈ 2.3333 ✓ |
| 3 | 216/175 | 41/175 | 175/41 ≈ 4.2683 | 427/100 = 4.27 ✓ (and 180/41 ✓) |
| 4 | 432/385 | 47/385 | 385/47 ≈ 8.1915 | 41/5 = 8.2 ✓ (and 390/47 ✓) |
| 5 | 5184/5005 | 179/5005 | 5005/179 ≈ 27.9609 | (none explicit in the paper) |
| 6 | 82944/85085 | **−2141/85085 < 0** | — | — |

I also confirmed the final identity `(5/4)·∏_{4≤j≤r+1} p_j/(p_j−1) · X_r = 9/5` for r = 2,3,4,5 and all the fraction comparisons above (trivial script; reproduce with `fractions.Fraction` + `sympy.prime`).

### CRITICAL FINDING: the theorem only yields bounds for 2 ≤ r ≤ 5

`X_r` is strictly decreasing in `r` (each new factor `(1 − 1/p) < 1`) and `X_6 = 82944/85085 < 1`. Hence, for **every r ≥ 6** the denominator `X_r − 1` of condition 1 is **negative** and:

- in the reading where the threshold is required to be positive (the intended reading, visible in the proof), **no** admissible pair `(A,B)` exists — the theorem is vacuous for r ≥ 6;
- in the literal reading (`A/B >` a negative number is trivially true), the statement would assert bounds that **the proof does not support**: the final step needs `1 + B/A < X_r`, impossible when `X_r < 1` (since `1 + B/A > 1`). Equivalently, the fixed part `(5/4)·∏_{4≤j≤r+1} p_j/(p_j−1) = 9/(5·X_r)` already exceeds `9/5` for r ≥ 6 (for r = 6 it is `1001·17/(576·16) = 17017/9216 ≈ 1.8465 > 9/5`), so the contradiction does not close for any choice of tail primes.

Conclusion: despite the title and abstract ("upper bounds for **each** of the prime divisors"), the method yields bounds **only for q_2, q_3, q_4 and q_5**. The real novelty vs. [4] is: (a) a unified parametric formulation in (A,B); (b) slightly better constants for q_3 and q_4 (Corollary 1.1); (c) a bound for **q_5** that was not in [4] — although the paper never states it explicitly as a corollary (admissible example: `(A,B) = (113,4)`, `113/4 = 28.25 > 5005/179`; for ω(n) = 10 it gives `q_5 < p_283 = 1847`). Label of this analysis: [PROVED] the infeasibility for r ≥ 6 in the paper's argument (exact arithmetic + monotonicity); we do not claim that the statements for r ≥ 6 are false — only that they are not proved there (and are vacuous/non-instantiable in the intended reading).

### Errata / minor remarks

- In the proof of Theorem 1.2, "From (3) and (4)" should read "From **(1)** and (4)" — (3) is an intermediate inequality of Case 1; what combines with (4) is the bound (1) on I(n). Harmless.
- Remark 1.1 presupposes `A > B > 1`; this follows from the conditions only when `T_r > 1`, which holds in the useful regime r ≤ 5 (T_r ≥ 25/11 > 2), but is not said explicitly in the statement of the theorem.
- The Conclusion suggests that, for fixed `ω(n) = m`, Theorem 1.2 would bound all m primes and it would remain to check "all combinations" — an overestimate: without bounds for q_6, …, q_m (and without exponent bounds), the search space remains infinite.
- The Conclusion cites (via OEIS A074902) that a friend of 10 is `> 10^30` (computational).

## 6. Fidelity to the overview table (docs/METHODOLOGY.md)

Row #6 of the table: *"Upper bounds for ALL prime divisors (generalisation of #5)"*.

- **Faithful to what the paper CLAIMS** (title/abstract say exactly that) and correct in calling it a generalisation of #5 (= arXiv:2404.05771, reference [4] here): Theorem 1.2 does recover the three theorems of [4] as instances and improves them for q_3, q_4.
- **Relevant DIVERGENCE:** what the theorem actually delivers does NOT cover all prime divisors. By the exact analysis of §5, admissible pairs (A,B) exist only for **r ∈ {2, 3, 4, 5}**; for r ≥ 6 the condition on A/B has a negative threshold and the proof does not close. Row #6 should read something like: "Parametric family of upper bounds for the r-th smallest prime divisor, effective for 2 ≤ r ≤ 5 (includes q_5, new, and better constants for q_3, q_4 than #5); for r ≥ 6 the method does not apply".
- Minor omission (inherited from the table's conciseness): the bounds are in terms of ω(N), of the form `q_r < ⌈Aω/B⌉(log + 2 log log)`, with the sharp form `q_r < p_{⌈Aω/B⌉}` available in the proof. Row #5 says "in terms of ω(N)" explicitly; row #6 does not, but the context ("generalisation of #5") covers it.

## 7. Use in the project

**Phase 1 (computational frontier):**
- Always use the sharp form `q_r < p_L` (not the log form) as pruning in the signature tree search: with ω(N) fixed at the node, the 5 smallest primes are confined to exact finite lists (for ω = 10: q_2 ≤ 83, q_3 ≤ 181, q_4 ≤ 419, q_5 ≤ 1846). All in exact integers/rationals — compatible with the rigour rules.
- Caution: q_6, …, q_ω remain without an upper bound by this method; tail pruning has to come from the exact rational `∏ I` (Ward/Nielsen style), as already planned in the roadmap.

**Phase 2 (new constraints):**
- Natural, cheap target: **extend to r ≥ 6** by replacing the fixed bound `q_j ≥ p_{j+2}` (worst case) with conditional information from the branch (if we know q_2 ≥ 11, the fixed product shrinks and X_r may become > 1 again for larger r). That is: turn the theorem into a pruning routine depending on the already-fixed prime prefix — a direct generalisation, probably publishable if made uniform.
- Replacing Rosser 1939 by finer bounds (Rosser–Schoenfeld 1962: `p_n < n(log n + log log n)` for n ≥ 6; Dusart) improves the constants for free, should the log form be needed in statements.
- Optimising (A,B): for each ω, the best L is essentially the least integer > T_r·ω compatible with conditions 2–4 (for r = 2 the condition `2A + B > B²` restricts B to small values; I checked that with ω = 10 one cannot beat L = 24). Gains are marginal; low priority.
- State and publish the corollary for q_5 explicitly (the paper did not) — a small, new, verifiable result, if nobody has published it (check the 2025–2026 papers of Mandal/Chatterjee first, per the novelty rule).

**Phase 3 (the 2p family):**
- The skeleton of the proof is fully reusable for a friend N of 2p (I(N) = 3(p+1)/(2p)): (i) basic facts about the least prime; (ii) bound `I < (fixed part) · (telescoping tail (L+ω−r)/(L−1))`; (iii) the parametric (A,B) trick with the two divisibility cases `B | ω`. The constant `36/25` becomes `(3(p+1)/(2p)) / (analogous fixed part)`; the same cut-off phenomenon (fixed part exceeding the target index from some r on) will appear and determine up to which r the uniform method reaches — worth formulating once for all p.

**Highlighted reusable technique:** the exact treatment of the ceiling `⌈Aω/B⌉` via `A = Bk + μ` and analysis of the fractional part `{μω/B}`, with monotonicity of the homographies `φ, τ` up to the limit `(A+B)/A` — avoids any asymptotic analysis and keeps everything in exact rationals; fits the project's rigour standard.
